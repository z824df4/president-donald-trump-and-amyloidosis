Does President Trump have amyloidosis? (June 2026)

Written as an FAQ. This is version 1.0.1.

What is the bottom line?

There are reasons to believe that President Trump has a disease called amyloidosis, with involvement of the heart, the blood vessels, and the nerves. The involvement of multiple organ systems helps fuel the suspicion of amyloidosis.

Although treatable, the disease has a poor prognosis. It limits exercise tolerance amd raises questions about his physical and mental stamina. Possibly his physicians have not considered he has amyloidosis because it has a reputation as an uncommon disease. But that is inaccurate. At autopsy, about 25-30% of people in their 70s and 80s have it.

To allay these concerns, President Trump should authorize his physicians to publicly discuss his medical condition in detail without restraint, answering questions in real time from medical reporters.

Who are you?

What is amyloidosis?

Amyloidosis occurs when the body makes too much of a specific protein, called "amyloid protein." Over decades the protein slowly infiltrates all the organs in the body and this impairs the function of each.

Some organs are more sensitive to this infiltration than others, and they show signs of dysfunction first. The long nerves going to the feet and hands are affected early. So are the small blood vessels in the skin, which become fragile and prone to breakage.

The heart is affected somewhat later. Infiltration of the heart makes the heart tissue stiff, which is bad because the mechanical efficiency of the heart depends on it being pliable. People with amyloidosis often die from their stiff heart.

Amyloidosis is difficult to diagnose because the disease moves slowly for a long time. The disease offers few clear signals that it is at work. People think they are just getting old.

Why do you think Trump has amyloidosis?

For all of these reasons, combined:

  1. He is old.
    Most cases of amyloidosis occur in older people.
  2. He has had large bruises on both hands for months.
    Bruises on the hands, without a clear traumatic cause, occur in amyloidosis because the infiltrated amyloid protein makes the walls of the blood vessels stiff. This stiffness makes them fragile and prone to breakage, resulting in bleeding and bruising.
  3. He has had prominently swollen ankles for months.
    In amyloidosis this is a sign that the heart has been infiltrated and is beginning to fail.
  4. He has had an unusual or cautious gait at times.
    This could be a sign of amyloidosis affecting the long nerves that go to the legs and feet. When these nerves do not function normally, people lose the instinctive feel for the position of the ground under their feet. This affects their gait and their habits of walking.
  5. He underwent a coronary angiogram in recent months.
    This would not have been done without a specific cardiac reason, because a coronary angiogram, even if done in a CT scanner, as Trump's was, carries non-zero risks of side effects, like kidney damage or a serious allergic reaction.
  6. He saw 22 specialists during his physical examination in May 2026.
    When amyloidosis is present, every organ in the body is eventually affected. If this happens to a person with good healthcare insurance, they end up seeing a multiplicity of specialists.

Isn't his ankle swelling due to chronic venous insufficiency?

Could be. He may very well have that condition. But is it the only condition he has?

Physicians often assume that an abnormality like swollen ankles has only one cause, but there is no law saying that must be true. Elderly persons like the 80-year-old Trump are especially likely to have multiple causes for an abnormal finding. For example, the high central venous pressures in amyloidosis can worsen chronic venous insufficiency.

It is sad that we must consider whether chronic venous insufficiency is a cover story or a misdiagnosis. This is a consequence of losing trust in Trump's medical team.

Are you saying chronic venous insufficiency is a cover story?

Yes and no. We must consider the meaning of the term.

"Chronic venous insufficiency" is an excellent diagnosis to use as a cover story because its meaning is remarkably wide-ranging. When used without elaboration, it is almost a useless term.

Why is chronic venous insufficiency, alone, a useless term?

Technically, the term can be used to mean nothing more than "swollen ankles."

The reasoning is this: if ankles swell, it means by definition that the veins are insufficiently draining all the fluid in the ankles. If swelling persists, it is "chronic," hence chronic venous insufficiency.

Because so many different conditions cause chronic ankle swelling (ranging from repeated ankle sprains to heart disease), this reasoning perverts "chronic venous insufficiency" into a clinically meaningless term that is medically a disservice to the patient... unless he is a politician wanting to project a public aura of vigor. But technically this perverted usage is not incorrect.

What term should his physicians be using?

The term "chronic venous insufficiency" is intended to describe patients who, from whatever cause, have longstanding bothersome symptoms as a result of poor fluid drainage from the lower limbs. But, because ankle swelling can be considered as one of those bothersome symptoms, we come full circle back to the beginning where longstanding ankle swelling all by itself can earn the label "chronic venous insufficiency."

For a patient with swollen ankles, therefore, the term "chronic venous insufficiency" becomes useful only when the cause of the swelling is named. Trump was not born with bad veins or lymphatics in his legs (some people are), so something has happened to make his ankles swell.

That something is the essential medical fact about Trump, but it has never been released. Maybe it is just his obesity. Maybe it is flabby veins in his legs. Maybe it is heart failure. Regardless, it is easy to believe that continually saying the fancy medical term "chronic venous insufficiency" without elaboration is a studious effort to avoid saying what that something is. The fancy medical term serves less as a cover story, but more as a distraction, that appears to answer the question of why his ankles are swollen without ever doing so.

He may. But, politically, one might expect medical reports to say so if it were the case. Medically, his history includes features that suggest otherwise.

Published photos show that the two ankles are swollen equally. Vein disease is often worse on one side, causing one ankle to be more swollen than the other. Vein disease can cause symmetric swelling, but it would have to be the same severity in both legs, and that is less probable. The symmetric swelling suggests something outside the legs is responsible.

Non-cardiac causes outside the legs could include blood clots, kidney disease, obstructive sleep apnea, and obesity. We'll concentrate on obesity, because the others would involve clear cover-ups.

How does amyloidosis cause swollen ankles?

By damaging the heart.

Amyloidosis in the heart makes the heart tissue stiff. This makes it hard for the heart to fill with blood between heart beats. It's like blowing into a stiff balloon compared to blowing into a pliable balloon: if you blow into both with the same force, the pliable balloon will better fill with air. You have to push with increased pressure to fill the stiff balloon.

Similarly, with a stiff heart, the body must push blood into it at a higher than normal pressure. This higher pressure is in the veins of the body. The higher pressure in the veins causes fluid to weep out, which gravity draws down to the feet, which causes visible swelling in the ankles and feet (at first).

So you suspect his heart is damaged?

Yes. Heart damage must be suspected in any person with non-traumatic swollen ankles.

As noted above and below, I do not completely accept the "chronic venous insufficiency" explanation for the swollen ankles.

Yes. An electron beam CT test in 2018 showed that he had enough calcium in his coronary arteries to qualify as "coronary artery disease." Chronic coronary artery disease can cause heart failure from ischemic cardiomyopathy.

The fact that he had a CT coronary angiogram in 2026 is also direct evidence of heart disease or cardiac complaints. Coronary angiography is never done as a screening check in asymptomatic non-neurotic people. What is the indirect evidence he has heart problems?

  1. Swollen ankles, as noted.
    This is a classic sign of the condition known as "heart failure."
  2. He had coronary angiography as part of his May 2026 evaluation.
    As noted earlier, this test would only be done if he voiced complaints that could indicate heart disease, or if someone around him noticed signs that could indicate heart disease, or if some other test disclosed a sign of heart disease.
  3. He appears to fall asleep at work, in the Oval Office.
    Disrupted sleep would make him sleepy during the day. Heart failure is notorious for disrupting sleep by two effects on breathing: orthopnea and central sleep apnea. (Look them up.) Central sleep apnea is different from the more common obstructive sleep apnea, and is difficult to treat.
  4. He is not physically active.
    By not physically active, I mean he does not break a sweat. Long hours at a desk, talking or writing, are not physical activity. People with heart problems often unconsciously reduce their physical activity to a level that does not provoke cardiac symptoms. Inactivity does not seem to be a recent phenomenon with Trump, but it is still important because it means that he is not active enough to precipitate warning signs of heart disease until the problem is far advanced.

Doesn't the proBNP level mean his heart function is normal?

No.

ProBNP is a normal protein found in the blood. In persons with heart failure, including those with heart failure caused by amyloidosis, the level of proBNP rises to abnormally high levels.

During his May 2026 exam, the amount of proBNP in Trump's blood waas normal. This would ordinarily rule out the possibility of significant heart failure. However, obesity can prevent the rise in proBNP seen in heart failure. Thus, Trump's normal value is useless in saying he is free of heart disease.

An additional minor point: Here I have used the incorrect name "proBNP" that is used in the May 2026 medical report. The correct name is NT-proBNP. There is an entire family of BNP proteins in the blood, and keeping them straight requires understanding. Technically, the commonly measured NT-proBNP is distinct from the rarely (if ever) measured proBNP. Using the wrong name is slangy and sloppy, and raises questions about his physicians' attention to detail and perhaps understanding of the physiology involved.

Which heart tests have not been disclosed?

We can identify them, based on long-established patterns of cardiac testing. The list is long.

Chest x-ray

The May 2026 does not mention a chest x-ray. It would be highly unusual, if not negligent, to omit a chest x-ray given all of the other cardiac testing that Trump has had.

The chest x-ray can show multiple factors related to heart failure, including the size of the heart and whether free fluid is collecting in the chest. It can sometimes show high pressures inside the veins of the lungs.

Electrocardiogram (standard)

A standard electrocardiogram was performed for the May 2026 examination, but its results were not reported publicly.

Instead, the results of an "AI-enhanced electrocardiogram" were reported, without saying whose AI was involved. There is no standard for AI electrocardiograms. It is still evolving as a technology. In marked contrast, every physician is trained to read a standard electrocardiogram because it tells so much about the heart and its diseases.

Amyloidosis of the heart can show up in standard electrocardiograms. "Low voltages" in the heart's electrical signals raise suspicions of amyloidosis. Conduction delays can also be seen.

Electrocardiogram (ambulatory)

This is mentioned only for completeness. Monitoring the heart rhythm for a period of weeks is simple and unobtrusive. An aggressive preventive medicine evaluation might include perform such a test, but specific symptoms, none of which Trump seems to have, are the usual trigger. There are no specific signs of amyloidosis that this might disclose.

Stress test

The May 2026 does not mention a stress test. Stress tests are commonly done in executive physicals.

If Trump had complained of chest discomfort or other ischemic symptoms he might have gone straight to angiography and bypassed a stress test. But Trump's coronaries were angiographically normal, which lowers the odds he complained of chest discomfort.

Exercise intolerance is another reason to perform a stress test, even if coronary angiography is planned. As a sedentary, obese, and elderly man, we expect that Trump's exercise performance would be embarrassingly poor. It is easy to sympathize with concealing those results. However, there are non-embarrassing stress tests using vasodilators and radioactive isotopes that do not involve physical exercise, and these are useful for quantifying cardiac performance in persons with exercise intolerance.

Amyloidosis of the heart would perhaps cause a reduction in cardiac performance during physiological stress. This would be a compelling finding that a competent physician would surely investigate.

Doppler echocardiogram

The May 2026 report says "Additional testing, including an echocardiogram, showed no abnormalities." The only other thing the report says about the echocardiogram is: "Cardiac chambers and valves are normal with a preserved ejection fraction."

This is good news, but is it the complete news?

Nothing in these remarks indicates that doppler studies were performed during the echocardiogram. Most echocardiograms today include doppler studies, but to a purist they do not. In fact, Medicare has different billing codes for doppler studies and echocardiography: as far as the US government is concerned, they are separate tests.

Doppler studies are important in amyloidosis because they show the filling pressures of the heart. Filling pressures are especially important in Trump's case, because they would clearly distinguish non-cardiac chronic venous insufficiency (normal filling pressures) from cardiac amyloidosis (high filling pressures).

We can be certain that Trump has had his filling pressures measured, because the evaluation of chronic venous insufficiency would otherwise be negligently incomplete. Therefore, it would have been the most natural thing in the world for the May 2026 report to mention normal filling pressures, and it would have been highly reassuring that Trump's heart is currently functioning well.

Doppler studies in amyloidosis are also exceedingly helpful because they measure the stiffness of the left ventricle, which is the fundamental issue when amyloidosis begins to affect the heart.

There is another sign of amyloidosis that careful readers of echocardiograms can see: a different texture to the walls of the left ventricle. This is very much a judgment call, so if a cardiologist wanted to convince themself that the texture was normal, that would certainly be possible in all but the most severe cases.

Overall, therefore, the echocardiographic results released are precisely the results we would expect to be normal in a case of cardiac amyloidosis: normal cardiac chambers, normal appearing valves, and a normal ejection fraction. None of the results released are those which could help inform the possibility of amyloidosis. These are disturbing coincidences.

Cardiac amyloidosis radionuclide imaging

This rarely performed nuclear medicine test is an accepted method to rule out (or rule in) the diagnosis of amyloidosis in the heart. It is performed only when the suspicion is high. Therefore, reporting any result from this test would admit that Trump was suspected of having cardiac amyloidosis. No doubt the White House would be unwilling to make this admission, even if the test result was completely normal.

How does amyloidosis affect walking?

When amyloid protein infiltrates the nerves in the legs and feet, a peripheral neuropathy results, meaning the nerves do not properly send sensory information up to the brain.

As a result, the patient loses the ability to instinctively tell where his feet are in relation to the ground.

The patient's gait therefore degrades. It may become unsteady or cautious.

Could amyloidosis be affecting Trump's gait?

Yes. But the possible causes for a cautious or unsteady gait in an 80 year old are vast. No specific diagnosis is possible from just that complaint.

The gait observations about Trump become important in the context of other evidence that he has amyloidosis. If Trump had amyloidosis affect the blood vessels of his skin (bruising) and affecting his heart, then abnormality of his gait would be an additional factor that would fit with a diagnosis of amyloidosis.

Anything else?

Protein in the urine is a common early sign of amyloidosis. A supposedly "comprehensive laboratory evaluation" of Trump reported in May 2026 does not mention a urinalysis, not even the non-embarrassing statement "Urinalysis unremarkable."

The blood level of alkaline phosphatase ("alkphos") rises with liver involvement in amyloidosis. Alkphos is routinely measured in blood tests alongside other liver tests such as the AST and ALT. In May 2026, Trump's AST and ALT levels were disclosed, but not alkphos.

Conclusions

This document expresses only a of suspicion amyloidosis in President Trump. It does not establish the diagnosis.

For anyone of President Trump's age, amyloidosis is a reasonable theory to explain bruises that look like his, ankles that look like his, and a gait that looks like his. These are medical concerns, not conspiracy theorizing. We have no insight into the thinking of Trump's many physicians.

If the information shared to date in the President's medical reports were assumed to be forthright, that would provide some reassurance that amyloidosis is not present. However, even if it were were forthright, the information released to date would be insufficient to allay concerns about amyloidosis, and so suspicion would remain. The suspicion is heightened by a repeated pattern in which the results of potentially exculpatory tests are not released, when it is clear they were performed. Therefore, the suspicions about amyloidosis await a full, free disclosure of the President's medical status.

By virtue of his office, President Trump is a man in whom all medical threats to his health should be investigated as aggressively as any threat of violence against him. Amyloidosis is a deadly disease that can be slowed down by medication, but not reversed. His medical team therefore has a duty to consider it, and to test for it as quickly as they can so that treatment can begin as quickly as possible. Physicians often do not entertain the possibility of amyloidosis until the disease is advanced. No doubt, Trump has good doctors on his team, so suggesting the amyloidosis possibility to them is probably unnecessary, but even if there is only a small chance of benefit, the suggestion is worth making due to the dangerous nature of the condition.

It is worthwhile to ask if the Vice President and Cabinet receive non-public information from Trump's physician. The 25th Amendment gives them the responsibility to interpret erratic presidential behaviors and decisions in a medical context. It is likely, but not proven, that amyloidosis directly affects cognitive performance. Heart failure as a complication of amyloidosis can certainly affect cognition and mental stamina. Regardless of the direct cause, cognitive effects would be subtle before becoming overt. Cabinet officials have a need to know to Trump's health information, so they can act before problems become overt and the nation suffers.
